The one-paragraph version

Banks hold their customers' consented financial data and still can't act on it fast enough. The bet is a bank-controlled advisory layer: a relationship manager asks a question in their own Copilot — "where is this client leaving working capital on the table?" — and Codat's Advisory Intelligence platform, reached through MCP, answers it against permissioned, harmonised accounting and banking data. Not AI-led underwriting. An insight and next-best-action layer for the people who advise the customer.

This is a discovery case study for the working-capital module of that platform. It's honest about status: the platform is real and demoed, and it isn't live with a bank yet. What follows is how we ran the discovery, who we spoke to, what they told us, and the calls that came out of it.

01 — Where this started

The advisor who can't advise

A relationship manager is meant to be a strategic advisor. Most of the week, they're a data janitor — chasing and cleaning fragmented client data instead of using it.

The picture a client needs to be advised on is split in two: the bank's own transaction ledgers on one side, the client's accounting or ERP system on the other. Stitching them together is manual — exports, spreadsheets, keyed-in figures — so recommendations arrive late, get pitched reactively after the client already asked, and can't be backed with a number. On the bank floor it shows up as speed: a working-capital need the bank can't respond to in time, and the revenue and the relationship both walk to a fintech.

How it works today

  • Client data split across the bank's ledgers and the client's ERP
  • Pulled together by hand — exports, spreadsheets, re-keying
  • Advisors sell reactively, after the client asks
  • Recommendations can't be quantified, so clients don't act
  • Working-capital needs lost on speed to a fintech

What the data makes possible

  • Bank and accounting data harmonised in one place
  • The RM asks in plain language and gets a grounded answer
  • Opportunities surfaced before the client goes looking
  • Recommendations sized, so the value is provable
  • Advice that fits the client's actual position

02 — The use case

An advisory layer, not an underwriter

The working-capital thesis is a relationship-manager insight layer — bank-side, on the bank's own AI tool, powered by consented data it can already reach.

An RM works inside the Copilot the bank already runs and asks, in their own words, where a client's working capital is trapped: DSO, DPO and DIO trends, AR ageing and customer concentration, what changed since last quarter and why. Codat sources and harmonises the financials; the bank's own relationship context meets it at the surface; Copilot does the final synthesis. The output is a pre-meeting brief, an identified opportunity, a sized recommendation, a drafted client note — the next-best action, grounded in the numbers.

The move it represents is from a bank that reacts to product requests to one that advises proactively across its book — and, for Codat, from selling data to answering questions with it.

The distinction that holds the product together

Underwrite on the API. Explore on the AI tool. The application is where one becomes the other.

Everything that lands on the record — a limit, a decision, a figure inside a model — goes through the API, where it's deterministic and reproducible. Everything before it — is this worth taking further, what should I lead with, what else does this client need — is a conversation, and that's where the AI tool earns its place. Keeping that line clear is what stopped the working-capital module from quietly turning into an underwriting one.

03 — How we ran the discovery

We treated it as discovery, not a pitch

The earlier version of this idea failed by exposing data without ever proving who used it or why. So we set a discipline: no module earns a build until the whole chain is proven.

METHOD

Built a discussion guide around three moves

How they work today, what actually hurts, and what they'd automate first. Thirty-to-forty-five-minute calls with relationship managers, treasury consultants and credit teams — plus non-bank lenders — walking each through a working-capital prototype to see where it plugged into their week.

PERSONA

Named the user and the job to be done

The relationship manager and treasury consultant at a commercial bank: someone under pressure to advise, stuck doing data assembly. The job is to diagnose where a client's cash is trapped, spot the opportunity, and bring a recommendation they can prove.

SURFACE

Tested where it fits in the workflow

Every prototype session probed the same fork: is this a portfolio scan across the whole book, or a client-level deep dive for one advisory conversation? Business-review prep, monitoring, or a one-to-one call? The answer shapes the product, so we made people choose.

GATE

Held every module to one commercial proof

ERP input → named user and workflow → bank outcome → measurable ROI. No proof, no build. It's the rule that keeps the roadmap from expanding on assumption and gives the MCP a governed catalogue of which modules exist and who they serve.

04 — What we heard

The signal was unusually consistent

Across banks and lenders the same themes came back — and in a couple of places they said our own thesis back to us, unprompted.

INSIGHT 01

They want answers, not raw data

Two non-bank lenders independently asked us to answer their questions and return insights — revenue and margin trends, cash-flow explanations, distress signals — rather than hand back records they dig through themselves. That's exactly how we'd framed the module, so hearing it come back unprompted was the strongest validation of the direction.

"Instead of low-level calls like get_transactions(), we'd like search_financial_data(prompt) — show revenue trends, explain the change in opex, identify signs of financial distress."— CTO, SMB revenue-based lender
Discovery · 2 lenders
INSIGHT 02

The value is the sources combined — with a domain-aware check

The useful layer isn't any one source; it's accounting, banking and financials reasoned over together. A lender already running its own AI over its data warehouse named the specific gap: checks raw data can't answer on its own — is the reconciliation clean, are the books actually closed through last month.

Discovery · venture-debt lender
INSIGHT 03

Nobody is ready to let AI make the decision

Everyone we spoke to already uses Copilot or Claude day to day — and none will let AI make the call yet. The early wins they name are the same two: portfolio monitoring, and the tricky one-off case. That maps straight onto the boundary — the decision stays deterministic; the exploration around it is where the AI tool belongs.

Discovery · 3 lenders
INSIGHT 04

The advisory pull is real — and points past the wedge

Bank stakeholders want a pull-based, holistic view across a client's relationships to open a review with — and nearly every senior person in the room asked for a portfolio / leadership view we don't yet show. We also heard an organic pull toward AR and working capital ahead of the card-led entry point, which reordered how we think about sequencing modules.

05 — The output

The question set we handed the platform

Discovery only counts if it turns into something buildable. We translated the working-capital job into the RM questions the MCP has to answer — and the data contract behind each one.

"Where is this client leaving working capital on the table?"

Needs: early vs late payments, DPO against terms, specific extend-or-discount opportunities.

"Give me DSO, DPO and DIO — and how they've trended over 12–24 months."

Needs: P&L, balance sheet, and inventory for the full cash-conversion cycle.

"Break down their AR ageing — current, 1–30, 31–60, 61–90, 90+."

Needs: AR aged buckets, derived from invoice due dates against today.

"Where's the biggest opportunity — receivables, inventory, or payables?"

Needs: a data-grounded answer specific to this client, not a generic benchmark.

"Show me their customer concentration and who's paying late."

Needs: outstanding invoices by counterparty, on-time behaviour, concentration risk.

One discipline that came straight out of the data

Inventory isn't present in every ERP — and without it, the cash-conversion cycle is incomplete. The call we made: the platform flags when it's missing rather than returning a confident-looking number that's quietly wrong. Trust is the whole basis of the pitch, and a fabricated CCC would spend it.

The open questions we're carrying

What still has to be true

Scope

Which modules make the cut, and which are Day-1. Narrowing to a handful, then the one-to-three for a lighthouse bank — and settling whether AR is its own module or folded into working capital.

Surface

The portfolio / leadership view isn't built. Every senior stakeholder asked for it and it's the thing that isn't demoed. The client deep dive is further along than the book-level scan.

The loop

Capturing outcomes isn't designed yet — the biggest hole. Questions and actions are logged through MCP; closing question → action → outcome is what compounds the platform, and it's still on paper.

Data

Expansion is mostly a consent and storage problem, not new integrations. Coverage is strong on the common ERPs, thin on the long tail — and inventory gaps break the cash-conversion cycle where they land.

Trust

Governance, consent and AI-governance sign-off gate deployment. The bank's central AI team holds the cards on how the pieces connect, and that approval runs longer than the field expects.

08 — Reflection

What we'd carry forward

The most useful thing we did wasn't a feature — it was insisting on the boundary. "Can we get this data into the AI tool" sounds like one request and is almost always two: the financials a banker wants to explore, and the scored output that feeds a decision. Splitting them early kept the discovery honest and stopped the working-capital module drifting into a job it isn't built for.

The second lesson is to sequence conviction and confidence separately. Conviction on the destination — bank-controlled advisory, on the bank's own AI, powered by consented data — is high and shared. Confidence on the dates falls away the further out you read, and it should. The job isn't to pretend otherwise. It's to prove one module's chain end to end, and let the rest earn their place one proof at a time.

And none of it moves at software speed. Getting one of the world's leading banks to yes means clearing its central AI team, its credit function and its consent process — any one of which can hold up the whole thing, and did, for longer than we expected. The surface isn't ours to move either: it runs on the bank's own Copilot, so the roadmap leans on Microsoft as much as on us. The hard part of reimagining what's possible was rarely the idea. It was aligning parties who each move on their own clock.
