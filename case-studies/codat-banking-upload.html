The challenge

Codat could only serve the businesses willing to connect a live feed

Codat's lending products turn a business's connected accounting and banking data into structured, categorised financials a lender can underwrite on. The problem: a large share of borrowers never connect at all — and the ones who won't are invisible to the entire product line.

The problem we kept hitting
  • ~60% of SMBs still share financials by PDF, email or post rather than linking their accounts
  • Even when linking is offered, only 20–40% of borrowers connect a bank account, depending on the partner
  • In the US, many banks return only 6 months of data via Plaid — too little for many underwriting decisions
  • Lenders bridge the gap with manual review plus a separate OCR tool — fragmented, slow, error-prone
→
The opportunity
  • Let an SMB upload a bank-statement PDF during the same Link journey they'd use to connect
  • Run it through Codat's existing pipeline so uploaded data is standardised, categorised and enriched like connected data
  • Reach the ~50% of businesses who won't link — one workflow for the lender, no second OCR vendor to build to
  • Extend a capability Codat already shipped for accounting data into banking, where the demand was loudest
Why it mattered commercially

We were already losing prospects over it: several walked because Codat couldn't ingest an uploaded statement, while Heron, Ocrolus, Perfios and Docsumo already could. As much about defending the roadmap as expanding it.

The outcome first

A contested idea, turned into a scoped, evidence-backed bet

The initiative was ambiguous and its ROI was openly challenged — it swung from green to red and back as the team pressure-tested scope and value. The discovery and feasibility work is what settled it: a clear, defensible recommendation to build a US-first MVP, currently in development for testing partners in early Q4.

The headline

"Can we read a bank statement well enough to underwrite on it?" — answered with a 71-statement benchmark, not an assumption.

Rather than take extraction accuracy on faith, I ran the OCR model against real statements across 30+ banks, proved it worked for US data, found and diagnosed the failure modes, and used that evidence to scope a realistic MVP. The commercial model and two prototyped journeys made the case concrete enough to fund.

How I got there

The process I ran

A discovery sequence built to attack the riskiest unknowns first. Feasibility was the one that could kill the whole idea, so that's where I went early — before design or a business case had anything to stand on.

01 · Frame

Frame the bet & the hypothesis

If we extract banking data from uploaded statements, lenders can support more SMB applications — because most borrowers prefer to share documents. Success = more connections per client.

02 · Demand

Size the demand

Borrower survey, competitor scan, a map of client interest and lost deals, and an ROI analysis I built with commercial — establishing this was a real, recurring need with a quantified opportunity, not one loud client.

03 · Feasibility

Benchmark the OCR

The make-or-break question. I tested the extraction model against 71 real statements to find out whether the data was actually good enough to underwrite on.

04 · Prove

Proof of concept

Turned the benchmark into a working PoC and a set of architecture decisions — how extraction, mapping and reconciliation should actually be built.

05 · Design

Prototype both journeys

Prototyped the borrower upload (in Link) and the lender review (in the Portal), grounded in the PoC's real behaviour and edge cases.

06 · Model

Build the commercial case

With GTM, a confidence-rated revenue model by client and a running-cost model — the numbers that moved the idea back out of red.

07 · Scope

Recommend & scope

A US-first recommendation with the UK deferred, cut into an MVP scope and engineering stories the squad could plan against.

→ · Result

Into build

Feasibility retired, scope defined, first engineering stories created — the bet moved from idea to delivery.

The make-or-break test · my own benchmark

I benchmarked the OCR before we committed a line of code

Extraction accuracy was the single biggest unknown — and the fastest way to waste a quarter. So I ran Azure Document Intelligence's prebuilt-bankStatement.us model against a real sample and measured what it could and couldn't do, grounded in Codat's own Banking: Transaction data model.

71

statements, 30+ banks

51 US and 20 UK statements spanning 2022–2026, deliberately skewed toward the SMB segments our lenders actually see.

10,717

transactions scored

Measured field coverage, amount-parse consistency, row-level confidence and the statement metadata a live feed can't give you.

~0

amount errors on US banks

Date and description coverage was high across the majority of US banks; the only material amount errors sat in one non-representative outlier layout.

The verdict

US works. UK needs a different model.

The headline was clean: pursue US statement processing; don't extend to the UK without a UK-tuned model. Extraction was strong across US banks, and the handful of apparent failures turned out to have understood root causes and simple post-processing fixes rather than model dead ends. The full analysis sits in the evaluation.

From benchmark to buildable

The architecture the PoC settled

The proof of concept wasn't just "does it extract?" — it was where the build decisions got made. The headline one: split extraction from mapping, so the mapping logic (which changes constantly) can be re-run without paying to OCR the document again.

Step 1

Extract

OCR the PDF into raw fields — dates, descriptions, amounts, balances and statement metadata. The output is stored, so it's produced once.

Azure Document Intelligence
Step 2

Map

A separate, re-runnable service maps raw output to Codat's contract: transaction dates, account type, reconciliation, grouping by account.

C# mapper · rules separated from ML
Step 3

Standardise

Uploaded data lands in the same Banking data model as connected data, so everything downstream treats it identically.

Codat Banking: Transaction
Step 4

Enrich

Codat's existing categorisation and Credit Model run on it — the whole point: an upload becomes as useful as a live connection.

Categorisation · Credit Model

Under it, the MVP decisions that fell out of the PoC: auto-group multi-file uploads by account, infer the year on dateless transactions, reconcile balances at statement level, and label the data honestly as sourced from PDFs.

The business case

The commercial model that moved it out of red

When the ROI was challenged, I built the case back up with GTM: a client-by-client revenue model with an explicit confidence rating on every line, set against a modelled running cost. The MVP is scoped to US processing — these are the US headline numbers.

Pricing · directional

A two-tier model

Usage~$10 per statement upload — the same shape as Lending API connectivity charges.
PlatformA per-annum product fee (~$10k+) for larger Tier 1 / Tier 2 clients, billed upfront and independent of ramp.
Running cost · modelled

Cheap to run, at any realistic volume

Azure Document Intelligence lists at ~$10 per 1,000 pages. Modelling 5,000 companies uploading quarterly gives a spread of roughly $6k–$18k per year depending on statement length — I took the high end as a deliberately conservative fixed cost.

The shape of the argument

One high-confidence design partner alone modelled at ~$310k of year-one ARR — against a running cost of ~$18k at the top end. Even discounting every low-confidence line to zero, the US case cleared comfortably. That's what turned a red initiative back to amber and into a funded scope.

De-risking the bet

Four big risks — and how discovery moved them

I assessed the opportunity against the four classic product risks and attacked the reddest first: feasibility was the most likely to be fatal, so the benchmark went before anything else. It retired feasibility for US data — and prototype testing then pulled usability and viability down to low as well, leaving no medium+ risk standing against the MVP.

Into delivery

Scoped, and moving into engineering

Discovery ended where it should — not in a deck, but in a scoped MVP the squad could start. The first engineering stories are cut straight from the PoC findings and the PRD's non-functional requirements.

Infrastructure

Provision the model, securely

Stand up Azure Document Intelligence in Terraform with a managed identity — inside Codat's VNET behind a private endpoint, no data retention, processing in-region, per the PRD's security requirements.

Mapping

Harden the extraction edge cases

The known failure modes become concrete stories: infer the year on dateless transactions, group multi-file uploads by account, reconcile balances at statement level.

Scope discipline

US-first, UK deferred

The MVP is deliberately US-only — the evidence supported it and the UK needs a different model. That kept the scope honest and the first release shippable.

Reflection

Getting the team comfortable with a moving target

Bank statements have no perfect answer — formats vary, confidence scores wobble, an upload never behaves like a clean API feed. The real work wasn't the benchmark; it was getting engineering comfortable building for that ambiguity instead of designing it away. I pushed the squad to ship the smallest thing we could test against real statements and treat "good enough to learn from" as the goal, not perfection.

The unlock was proximity to the client. I ran a deep workflow call with a large fintech and opened it to the whole squad — how they handle manual uploads, their own OCR and its accuracy, where underwriters step in. For engineers who'd only seen the data model, watching a real workflow made the problem concrete and the ambiguity testable.

What moved the team

One deep-dive call did more for shared conviction than any spec — the people building it heard the questions and saw the friction first-hand.

"It helped me a lot more seeing a lender's workflow — it's given us great first-level insight into the problems we're trying to solve."
