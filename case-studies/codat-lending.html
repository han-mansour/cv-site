Where this started

A ways-of-working problem, not a feature gap

When I picked up the lending team, the roadmap was whatever the loudest client had asked for in the last sales call. This piece of work was the shift out of that — from reacting deal by deal to a product-led view of where lending could actually grow.

When I picked it up
  • Sales-led — the roadmap followed sales conversations, not customer discovery
  • A narrow, feature-level product remit rather than a strategic one
  • Priorities set by the loudest client, one deal at a time
→
Where I moved it
  • Product-led — grounded in discovery, client data and the whole lifecycle
  • Zoomed out to the broadest lending opportunities, not one client's ask
  • A shared strategy the team owned, so priorities came from evidence, not volume
The outcome first

What the work produced

A shared, evidence-backed view of where Codat should invest to grow adoption of its lending products — expressed as an opportunity solution tree, and narrowed to a handful of H2 bets that engineering and GTM signed up to.

The headline

One question — "where's the highest-leverage place to invest in lending?" — answered with the whole picture, not a hunch.

Rather than jump to a feature, I held the team in the problem space: map the lending lifecycle, understand each persona, read the client data, cluster the pain, then let solutions fall out of the opportunities. The tree became the artifact the squads planned H2 against.

How I got there

The process I ran

A discovery sequence, not a single meeting. Each step narrowed the space — from "why are we even looking at this?" to a tree the team could act on.

01 · Frame

Frame the opportunity

Set the goal (grow adoption of lending products), the metric (billable units / active connected companies), and the real question: where's the highest-leverage place to invest? Problem space before solutions.

02 · Discover

Cross-functional discovery

Ran a workshop with engineering, implementation and GTM, paired with client interviews and product-usage data — so feasibility, client reality and commercial signal were all in the room.

03 · Map

Map the lending lifecycle

Laid out the seven stages from pre-qualification to renewal, borrower ↔ lender, so every pain point had a place on the journey.

04 · Personas

Personas & job stories

Wrote "when… I want… so I can…" job stories for borrower and lender roles, grounding the abstract workflow in real motivations.

05 · Synthesise

Cluster the pain

Grouped hundreds of pain points and quotes into a handful of themes that recurred across segments — the signal under the noise.

06 · Prioritise

Two prioritisation workshops

Ran a value/effort pass with engineering and a business-value vs. client-value pass with commercial — two lenses that had to agree before a bet made the shortlist.

07 · Shape

Strategy & solution tree

Turned the prioritised opportunities into an opportunity solution tree and an H2 focus the squads could plan and delivery against.

→ · Result

A plan people owned

Because eng and GTM built the tree with me, the H2 focus landed as a shared decision — not a doc handed over a wall.

Who's in the workflow

The people on both sides

Five roles kept surfacing. Naming them — and what each is really trying to do — stopped us designing for an average user who doesn't exist.

Lender

Relationship Manager / AE

Qualifies the deal, sends the data request (embedded flow or a portal link), and chases what's missing.

"When a prospect asks 'could you fund X?', I want a directionally-accurate answer in the first meeting."
Lender

Credit Analyst / Underwriter

Spreads statements, builds the memo, defends it in committee. Lives or dies by whether the mapped data can be trusted.

"When I review an application, I want structured, mapped financials — not a zip of PDFs."
Lender

Credit / Risk exec buyer

COO, head of lending, credit-risk lead. Cares about ROI, default rates, and the trade-off between speed of underwriting and risk.

"I want to balance faster decisions against the losses a wrong one creates."
Lender

Lender Product Manager

Our main point of contact — wires Codat into each lending product and the underwriting tech stack.

"I want the data flowing cleanly into the tools my underwriters already use."
Borrower

SME borrower

Running a business, not a finance function. Wants to share data once and get on with the day.

"When my RM asks for financials, I want to share them once, quickly, and get back to work."
The tension

Speed vs. trust

Borrowers want minimal friction; lenders need audit-defensible, complete, fresh data. Almost every opportunity lives in closing that gap.

The workflow only improves when both sides win at once.

What we heard

Six themes under the noise

Hundreds of stickies and quotes clustered into a handful of problems that showed up at almost every lender, whatever their size.

01

Data collection is the bottleneck

Weeks lost chasing borrowers for piecemeal, wrong-format or incomplete documents — before analysis even starts.

02

Spreading is slow and manual

Analysts re-key figures from PDFs. They want structured, mapped financials they can trust, not a folder of files.

03

Categorisation accuracy caps automation

Not accurate "enough" for the largest clients to remove the human in the loop — and recategorising is clunky.

04

Monitoring runs on stale data

Annual accounts mean a borrower's decline can surface months late — long after a lender could have acted.

05

Borrowers resist live connections

Security and privacy fears mean many would rather upload a file than connect a live feed — a coverage gap and an opportunity.

06

Coverage has hard edges

Not every platform is integrated. Widening the ways data can come in reaches more borrowers, faster.

"When a customer sends a bank statement, it's a PDF. We'd want the revenue categorised from that — not only from an API connection."

— Embedded-finance lender

"Many customers will upload PDFs rather than connect. There's real value in matching that to API-grade data quality."

— Invoice-finance fintech

"It could save 25–30% of the time we spend keying figures in by hand."

— Fintech lender (Credit Model)
Narrowing down · two workshops

From themes to bets

I didn't prioritise alone. I ran two workshops with different lenses — one with engineering on feasibility, one with commercial on value — so the shortlist held up to both "can we build it?" and "will it pay off?".

How commercial scored business value

Does it drive revenue — more connections (usage) or new charges, framed as new vs. existing and win vs. save — and / or is it a strategic foundation for the insights product line?

…scored separately from client value

Does it solve a real client or end-user need they'll actually adopt? Deliberately kept apart from revenue, so a commercially tempting bet with weak user pull couldn't hide.

Commercial workshop · business value × client value

What rose to the top

The bets in the top-right — high business value and real client pull — were the ones we committed to. Engineering's feasibility read then sequenced them: two "big rocks" scored high but heavy, so they sat behind the quick wins rather than getting dropped.

  • Quick, high-reach wins — intelligent uploads (banking first) and exposing existing credit indicators & risk signals via API.
  • Strategic foundations — categorisation accuracy underpins every downstream insight, so it earned investment despite the heavier build.
  • Strategic, with caveats — multi-entity analysis (a route into larger banks) and insights from past decisions: high business value, flagged for legal & data questions first.

Reflection

Why this worked

The discipline was staying in the problem space long enough. It's tempting to open with "let's build intelligent uploads" — but the workshop earned a strategy the team believed in, because they watched it come out of the evidence with me.

The tree also gave us a shared language: engineering could see where they were exposing existing value versus building new; GTM could see which client asks were served; and I had a prioritised H2 that survived contact with delivery.

What I did next
  1. Sequenced the big rock — categorisation accuracy — into a discovery track behind the quick, high-reach wins.
  2. Set a banking-data focus for Q3 and accounting-data for Q4: one clean strategy the cross-functional team bought into, and carried it into client roadmap and discovery calls.
  3. Shipped two banking-data enhancement features and started a third — bank-statement PDF upload / OCR — before the end of Q3.
