Codat · Product Case Study, 2026

Reasoning from the ground up

A first-principles case study on why Codat's lending categorization accuracy was under strain.

The problem

When the data science team that had originally built Codat's categorization models left the business, institutional knowledge of how those models actually worked left with them. No one had a reliable view of what the system cost to run or how well it was performing — a gap that made the underlying issues difficult to diagnose, viable improvements nearly impossible to identify with confidence, and the value of any fix hard to quantify.

Method

Every claim was tested by how it was earned. Facts are supported by direct evidence. Assumptions are believed but unverified. Conventions are habits carried from precedent rather than evidence. Only what survived scrutiny against the facts was allowed to inform the recommendation.

Client and individual names have been anonymized to protect confidentiality; the underlying findings are unchanged.

Where this sits in the pipeline

Categorization isn't a standalone feature — it's shared infrastructure. Raw accounting and banking data is standardized, run through the two models this case study examines, then consumed by every credit and reporting surface built on top. That shared position is exactly why the accuracy question was worth investigating formally: a flaw here doesn't stay contained, it propagates downstream.

The facts

6 evidence-backed findings

Lenders reported that bank transaction categorization (BTC) accuracy fell short

Fintech Lender A · Fintech Lender B · Fintech Lender C · Fintech Lender D
evidence
Lender A found the output underwhelming, with little usable context on checks and transfers, and began evaluating alternative providers. Lender B built its own bank-transaction model in-house, citing higher accuracy than any third-party provider it had tested. Lender C ran a successful proof of concept with a specialist competitor. Lender D, a strategically important renewal, disclosed it was building an internal alternative — making an accuracy improvement time-critical to retaining the account.

Lenders reported that chart-of-accounts categorization (CoA-Cat) wasn't good enough

Fintech Lender E · Fintech Lenders F, G, H · Fintech Lender C
evidence
Lender E: "the categorizations are too granular and are often inaccurate." Lenders F, G and H each maintained entirely separate tagging workflows in spreadsheets, cross-referenced manually against the platform's output. Lender C built its own fallback because a required expense split (operating vs. non-operating) wasn't modelled, which affected the accuracy of its downstream reporting.

Internal confidence in the ML model's own benchmarks was low

No drift monitoring · no dedicated DS/MLOps resourcing · original team no longer in place
evidence
In one review of a recent training run, Level 1 scores looked implausibly high (~99.9% precision/recall), as did the combined Level 3–5 score (~95%). The data science team flagged this as a warning sign rather than a result to celebrate — scores that clean usually indicate a flawed test set, not a strong model.

Recategorization wasn't built around how clients actually worked

Only 54 of 86 clients recategorized anything in 2025
evidence
Lender J chose a competitor partly for its stronger recategorization flow. Large Bank A couldn't use the workflow at all, since it recategorizes only after underwriting is complete. Confirmation rates were close to zero even among clients who did recategorize (Large Bank C: 0.42%, Neobank A: 0.12%). Lender I's team preferred working directly in a spreadsheet over the in-product workflow.

Categorization is a genuinely difficult problem to solve well

BTC is structurally harder than CoA-Cat
evidence
Bank transactions carry thin, inconsistent context and constantly shifting merchant names — a moving target. A chart of accounts is comparatively stable, set up once by the business. Even specialist competitors focused solely on bank-transaction data hadn't fully solved it.

Any material taxonomy change would disrupt existing clients

Several clients, including Lender D and Large Bank B, automate on today's taxonomy
evidence
Clients had built automated workflows on top of the current taxonomy; changing it would mean months of rework on their side. A migration project would need to clear a high bar of value to justify that disruption.

Assumptions

believed, not verified

Improving accuracy is the highest-leverage fix for revenue

The internal go-to-market view, cited in several lost sales opportunities
  • Lender D and Lender B had already built their own categorization — would improved accuracy bring them back?
  • Lender E's own workaround (reprocessing exports with an LLM) might not disappear even with higher accuracy.

Clients mainly want higher accuracy on the existing taxonomy

Clients consistently said the output "wasn't good enough"
  • The evidence pointed to taxonomy gaps (Lender C's missing expense subcategories) and excessive granularity, not accuracy alone.
  • Lender K's own model used a single, flat level — the opposite of more granularity.

Accurate categorization is required for the broader BCE / MCP product strategy

Based on an internal claim that granular (Level 3) accuracy sat at 66%
  • Internal benchmarks weren't trusted enough to treat that figure as a reliable signal.

Conventions

habit and precedent, not evidence

"Quick wins" would improve CoA-Cat accuracy

An analysis identified four fixes, reporting 88.5% accuracy
  • The fixes were validated on the same sample of accounts used to derive them — a circular test that said nothing about accuracy elsewhere.

Simplifying the taxonomy would help adoption

The 250+ category, five-level taxonomy read as inherited from the original model design, not derived from client need
  • Lender C's request was a taxonomy gap, not a case for simplification.
  • Client categorization needs varied enormously by industry.

Model trust should be rebuilt before doing anything else

The intuitive first step
  • Proposed previously and shelved — doing it properly would require significant time and resource that hadn't been allocated.

Where that left us

  • The available evidence didn't establish definitively what was wrong — much of the internal documentation reflected hypothesis rather than root cause.
  • There was no internal confidence in the ML model's benchmarks or accuracy.
  • Categorization needs varied enormously across lending clients, several of which had already built their own layer on top of the platform's output.
  • Larger banking clients had already standardized on established third-party taxonomies of their own.

therefore

Recommendations

Build

Establish a shared view of model performance

A documentation exercise, not new analysis: pull the existing accuracy figures for both models into one dashboard and reference document, each figure dated and sourced. Flag explicitly where figures are stale or missing, and separate genuine measured accuracy from confidence scores being used as a stand-in for it.

Scope: existing figures only, no new modelling · Target: end of Q3
Decision

Do not invest further in BTC

Specialist competitors focused solely on this problem hadn't solved it either. This was not a space to win with a partial, side-of-desk effort.

Decision

Do not change the CoA-Cat taxonomy at this time

No clear path to improvement existed that didn't require a client migration effort disproportionate to the expected value — including a move to a third-party standard taxonomy.

Revisit if: new client feedback pointed to specific improvements, or clearer patterns emerged as the product strategy evolved.
